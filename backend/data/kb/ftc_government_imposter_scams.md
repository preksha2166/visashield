## How government imposter scams start
Government imposter scams usually begin with a call, email, text or social media message from someone who says they work for an agency. Examples include the FTC, the Social Security Administration, the IRS or Medicare. To seem real, scammers may give a fake "employee ID number" and may already know your name or address. They then say something bad will happen unless you pay or share personal information, or that you will miss out on a benefit.

## Payment methods only scammers demand
The FTC explains that government imposters insist on payment methods that are hard to trace and hard to reverse. These include wire transfers, gift cards, cryptocurrency, payment apps and cash. Scammers choose these methods because once the money is sent it is very difficult to get back. A demand to pay a government "fine" or "fee" in any of these ways is a clear sign of a scam.

## What real government agencies will not do
The FTC lists things real agencies will not do. They will not call, email, text or message you on social media to demand money or personal information. They will not threaten you with arrest or legal action to make you pay. They will not demand payment by wire transfer or gift card. They will not ask for your Social Security, bank account or credit card numbers in an unexpected contact, or suspend benefits or licenses over the phone.

## Caller ID can be faked
Your caller ID may show a government agency's real name or phone number, such as "Social Security Administration". The FTC warns that caller ID can be faked. The person calling could be anyone, anywhere in the world. A familiar number on the screen is not proof that a call is real. If a call or message might be genuine, hang up and call the agency yourself using a number from its official website.

## What to do when contacted
The FTC advises people to hang up or ignore suspicious contacts. Do not give financial or personal information to someone who contacts you out of the blue. Do not click links in unexpected messages; delete them instead. To check whether there is a real problem, contact the agency directly using a number you know is correct. If a robocall asks you to press a number to speak to someone, do not press anything.

## Where to report government impersonation
Report government imposter scams to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. You can also report them to your state attorney general, whose contact details are listed through naag.org. Reports help the FTC and its law enforcement partners find and stop scammers. If you shared personal information such as your Social Security number, go to IdentityTheft.gov. It gives step-by-step recovery and protection advice based on what was exposed, so you can act before the information is misused.

## "Move your money to keep it safe" is a scam
A January 2024 FTC alert describes scammers who pose as bank fraud staff, the FTC, Amazon or government agencies. They claim there is suspicious activity on your account or that someone is using your identity. Then they urge you to move money to a "safe" account, into cryptocurrency or into gold to protect it. Real financial institutions will never ask you to move money to protect it. Check any warning by calling the number on your card or statement.

## Fake agents sending photo IDs and badges
In June 2026, the FTC warned about scammers who pose as FTC employees and promise to recover money lost in earlier scams. To gain trust, they text photos of fake employee ID cards and badges. The FTC says its real employees do not contact people by text or messaging apps like WhatsApp. They do not send photos of their IDs, and they do not charge money to recover scam losses. A badge photo sent over chat is not proof of identity.

## Scale of imposter scams in 2025
The FTC reported in June 2026 that people said they lost $3.5 billion to imposter scams in 2025. Reported losses to government impersonators were about $920 million, up from $789 million in 2024. Overall fraud losses reported to the FTC were about $16 billion, up about 25% from 2024. Scammers reached people by text, phone, email, social media and search results. The FTC repeated that it will never demand money, make threats, tell you to transfer money, or promise a prize.
Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/06/ftc-data-show-people-reported-losing-3-point-5-billion-imposter-scams-2025

## Government imposter reports keep rising
A May 2026 FTC alert said the agency received more than one million imposter scam reports in 2025. It was the ninth year in a row that imposter scams were the most reported fraud. Reports of government imposter scams went up about 40%. Many of these were fake toll-payment texts that threatened late fees or registration suspension. The FTC advises contacting the real agency using contact details you look up yourself, not details from the message.
