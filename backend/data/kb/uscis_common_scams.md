## Government impersonators
Scammers pretend to be USCIS officials to frighten immigrants and visa holders into paying money or handing over personal information. USCIS states that it contacts people only through official government channels. It does not reach out through a person's private social media accounts. A message that claims to be from USCIS but arrives through a personal Instagram, Facebook, WhatsApp or similar account should therefore be treated as a scam until it is confirmed through an official USCIS channel.

## How USCIS accepts payments
USCIS takes online payments only through a person's official USCIS online account and through Pay.gov. It will never ask anyone to send money to an individual person. USCIS does not accept Western Union, MoneyGram, PayPal, Venmo or gift cards. USCIS also says it will not ask for payment over the phone or by email. Any caller or emailer who wants an immigration fee, fine or penalty paid through one of these methods is not USCIS.

## Official email addresses and websites
Email addresses belonging to the US government always end in ".gov". USCIS warns that scammers set up fake USCIS social media pages, fake emails and fake websites that copy the agency's look. A sender address or link that ends in something other than .gov, such as a free webmail account or a .com or .org site using the USCIS name, is a warning sign. USCIS runs official social media accounts on X (Twitter), Instagram, YouTube, Facebook and LinkedIn.

## Student scams
USCIS lists scams aimed at foreign students. These include fake universities and threatening phone calls made to international students. Callers may claim there is a problem with a student's immigration record and push for quick payment or personal details. Students who get an unexpected call like this should not pay or share information. They should check with their school's international student office or with USCIS through official channels.

## Job offer scams
Fake employers may offer jobs, often overseas, and ask for money up front before the "job" starts. USCIS lists this among its common scams. The "employer" may promise that the job will lead to a visa, work permit or other immigration benefit. A job offer that requires the applicant to pay first, especially one tied to an immigration benefit, is a strong sign of fraud. Real employers do not sell jobs to applicants.

## Form I-9 email scams
USCIS warns of phishing emails that pretend to come from USCIS and ask employers for employment eligibility verification (Form I-9) information. These messages use USCIS branding to look real. A student starting an OPT job may see similar requests passed on by an employer. Employers and employees should confirm any such request through official USCIS channels before replying or sending documents. Suspicious USCIS-branded emails can be forwarded to USCIS.Webmaster@uscis.dhs.gov.

## "Jumping the line" and expediting schemes
Some businesses falsely claim they have special connections at USCIS. They say they can speed up a case or skip the normal processing line if the applicant pays a fee. USCIS lists this as a scam. Students waiting on an OPT or STEM OPT decision may be tempted by such offers, but the claim of a private "inside contact" who can move a case faster for money is itself a warning sign. Case status can be checked for free through official USCIS tools.

## Notarios publicos fraud
In many Latin American countries, a "notario publico" is a lawyer. In the United States, notaries public are not authorized to give legal services. USCIS warns that unlicensed notaries and consultants sometimes falsely claim they can give immigration legal advice or file cases. Only licensed attorneys or accredited representatives may give immigration legal help. Before paying anyone for immigration help, check that they are a licensed attorney or an accredited representative.

## Visa lottery and other program scams
The State Department runs the diversity visa lottery and will never email someone to say they were selected. USCIS also says it does not routinely send emails announcing diversity visa approvals. Scammers also target humanitarian programs by charging fees that do not exist. For example, USCIS does not charge a fee to file Form I-134A, and scammers have demanded fees before TPS re-registration notices were even published.

## Where to report a USCIS-related scam
USCIS recommends reporting suspected immigration scams to the Federal Trade Commission. Suspicious emails that pretend to be from USCIS can be forwarded to USCIS.Webmaster@uscis.dhs.gov. Victims of coerced labor or human trafficking can call the National Human Trafficking Hotline at 888-373-7888. USCIS also lists its official social media accounts, so a student can check whether a page or profile using the USCIS name is genuine before trusting a message sent from it.
