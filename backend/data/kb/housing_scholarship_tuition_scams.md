## How rental listing scams work
The FTC describes two main rental scams. In one, scammers copy a real listing's photos, description or virtual tour and put their own contact details on it. In the other, they post an ad for a place that does not exist or is not for rent. They may take application fees, deposits or rent. They may also collect Social Security and driver's license numbers for identity theft, or push victims to sign up for paid credit monitoring.

## Rental scam red flags
Warning signs include rent far below the going rate, pressure to decide quickly, and a "landlord" who cannot meet or show the property, often because they say they are out of the country. Others are demands for payment by wire transfer, gift card or cryptocurrency, and requests for personal information before any lease is agreed. A listing for a home that is actually for sale, not for rent, is another warning sign the FTC mentions.

## Protecting yourself when renting
Search the address online together with the owner's or company's name. If the same place is listed under different names, that is a warning sign. Search the owner's name with "scam", "complaint" or "review". Visit the property, or ask someone you trust to visit it, before signing or paying. Get the contract before sending a deposit. Do not give your SSN or credit details until the rental terms are agreed. Report rental scams to local police, the listing website, ReportFraud.ftc.gov and your state attorney general.

## Scholarship scam warning signs
The FTC says scholarship scams often begin with a social media post, email or letter saying you have been "selected" for a scholarship or aid package. Some invite you to a high-pressure seminar. Never pay to apply for a scholarship. Typical scam lines include claims that the scholarship is guaranteed or your money back, that you only need to give a card or bank number to "hold" it, or that you are a "finalist" in a contest you never entered.
Source: https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-avoid-scholarship-and-financial-aid-scams

## Financial aid "processing fee" scams
Some companies charge fees to get students grants, loans or work-study, which are free to apply for. Some even fill in the FAFSA with false information, which can expose the student to penalties of up to $20,000 or prison. The FTC advises completing the FAFSA yourself, never sharing your FSA ID, and asking your school's financial aid office before paying anyone. Scholarship and aid scams can be reported at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to your state attorney general.
Source: https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-avoid-scholarship-and-financial-aid-scams

## Tuition payment scams aimed at international students
Flywire, an international tuition payment company, warns about scams aimed at international students. Some scammers offer discounted tuition payments or offer to pay tuition for the student. Others pose as government agents and threaten visa problems unless a payment is made right away. Some approach students on campus, in visa lines or at admitted-student events. Others make documents with fake co-branded logos claiming a link to a university. Fake job offers may also recruit students as "money mules" who move funds for criminals.
Source: https://www.flywire.com/resources/six-steps

## Paying tuition safely
Flywire's advice is to pay only through secure (https) sites and providers your school has confirmed. Do not share login details with payment processors you have not verified. Check who is asking before giving sensitive information. Be skeptical of offers that seem too good to be true, such as discounted tuition. Report suspected fraud to both your university and the police. The main message is to check any payment processor directly with your school before using it.
Source: https://www.flywire.com/resources/six-steps
