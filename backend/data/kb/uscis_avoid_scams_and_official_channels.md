## What USCIS will never do
USCIS says it will not ask anyone to transfer money to an individual person. Fees are not paid outside the official USCIS process, such as through your USCIS online account. USCIS forms are free to download from the official website. A person who offers to sell you a USCIS form, or asks you to pay a private person to "process" a USCIS filing, is not acting for USCIS.

## You do not need a paid middleman
USCIS points out that you do not need a representative to file forms. People can file forms and represent themselves. If you want help, only licensed attorneys or accredited representatives of organizations recognized by the Department of Justice may represent you. USCIS advises checking a representative against the official lists before hiring them. Offers to speed up a case for a fee, or promises of quick benefits, are red flags.

## Trust only .gov websites
USCIS tells people to trust only official .gov websites and communications. Scammers often build sites on .com, .org, .net or .info addresses that look like government pages. Emails with spelling mistakes, unexpected attachments, or offers to expedite a case for money are common signs of a scam. Scammers also try to make the immigration process seem more complicated than it is so that victims will pay for "help".

## Official USCIS email sender
On June 18, 2024, USCIS changed its email update service. Messages now come from uscis@messages.dhs.gov. The old sender domain, public.govdelivery.com, is no longer used. USCIS said the change was made to strengthen security and help people confirm that messages are real. An email claiming to be a USCIS update that comes from a free webmail account or a non-government domain is suspicious.
Source: https://www.uscis.gov/i-9-central/form-i-9-related-news/uscis-updates-have-a-new-email-address

## What real USCIS case notifications contain
When someone files Form G-1145, USCIS can send an email or text saying it has accepted an application. These e-Notifications include the receipt number and explain how to get case status information. They do not include personal information, not even the person's name, because email and text are not secure. A real notification will not ask you to reply with personal data, pay a fee, or call a private phone number.
Source: https://www.uscis.gov/g-1145

## Checking a case status yourself
You can check a case status yourself with USCIS Case Status Online, reached through uscis.gov. A USCIS receipt number has 13 characters: three letters, such as EAC, WAC, LIN, SRC, NBC, MSC or IOE, followed by 10 numbers. A free USCIS online account at my.uscis.gov shows recent case actions. The USCIS Contact Center number is 1-800-375-5283. Checking status this way is safer than trusting a link or phone number in a message.
Source: https://www.uscis.gov/tools/checking-your-case-status-online

## Spoofed calls impersonating USCIS
USCIS warns that scammers use technology to make their caller ID look like a government agency or another trusted number. These callers may ask about your immigration status or case and try to get personal or financial information. Real USCIS staff will not pressure you to act immediately or threaten you with consequences. If you are unsure, hang up and contact USCIS through its official channels.
Source: https://www.uscis.gov/save/current-user-agencies/news-alerts/fraud-alert-reports-of-scam-calls-impersonating-uscis

## Where to report immigration scams
USCIS suggests several places to report. Report to your state consumer protection office or attorney general, and to the Federal Trade Commission at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. If you lost money or property, also report to local police. Fake websites or social media accounts that claim to be part of USCIS can be reported by email to uscis.webmaster@uscis.dhs.gov. Many states also let people report scams anonymously.
Source: https://www.uscis.gov/scams-fraud-and-misconduct/avoid-scams/report-immigration-scams

## Reporting will not hurt your case
USCIS states that reporting a scam will not affect your immigration application or petition. This matters for international students, who may fear that contacting authorities will put their status at risk. Phishing emails pretending to be USCIS can be forwarded to USCIS.Webmaster@uscis.dhs.gov. Fraud by people posing as immigration legal providers can also be reported to the Executive Office for Immigration Review fraud program.
