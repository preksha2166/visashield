## Never pay to get a job
The FTC's main rule for job seekers is simple: do not pay for the promise of a job. Honest employers, including the federal government, will never ask you to pay to get a job. Fees for "training", "certification", "starter kits", equipment or guaranteed placement are signs of a scam. The FTC also notes that honest placement firms do not usually charge job candidates. Information about federal and Postal Service jobs is free to everyone.

## Common job scam formats
The FTC describes several common job scams. Work-from-home offers promise thousands of dollars a month for little effort, then sell useless kits or training. Reshipping jobs, often called "quality control" or "virtual assistant" roles, have you receive packages bought with stolen cards and send them on, sometimes overseas, which makes you part of a fraud. Other scams include fake mystery shopper jobs, reselling luxury goods, and fake nanny, caregiver or assistant postings.

## How to check a job offer
Before accepting a job, the FTC suggests searching the company name with words like "scam", "review" or "complaint". Talk the offer over with someone you trust. Refuse any request to pay up front. Never deposit a check from an employer and then send part of the money somewhere else. Good places to look for jobs include USAJobs.gov, CareerOneStop and USA.gov. Report job scams to ReportFraud.ftc.gov or to your state attorney general.

## What a task scam is
Task scams, also called gamified job scams, start with an unexpected text, WhatsApp, Telegram or social media message. It offers easy online "work" such as "optimization" or "product boosting". You complete tasks in an app or website and see a "commission" for each click. The scammer may pay a small amount, often $5 to $20, so the job seems real. The growing earnings shown in the app are fake.
Source: https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2025/08/how-spot-and-avoid-task-scams

## The task scam trap: deposit to unlock earnings
At some point the scammer says you must deposit your own money, usually in cryptocurrency, to unlock the next set of tasks or withdraw your earnings. Even after you deposit, the money cannot be withdrawn, and your real money is gone. The FTC advises ignoring unexpected job messages on text, WhatsApp or Telegram, because real employers will not recruit that way. Never pay to get paid. No honest company pays people just to like or rate things online.
Source: https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2025/08/how-spot-and-avoid-task-scams

## Job offer texts in 2026
An April 2026 FTC alert described fake recruiters who text people claiming to work for well-known companies. They offer remote jobs such as "online assessor" or a vague "remote position". They often state a daily or weekly pay rate but give no real details about the work. Instead of sending a link, they ask you to reply "YES" or "INTERESTED". Later they ask for money, through either a fake check you must partly pay back or tasks that require a deposit.
Source: https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2026/04/job-offer-text-probably-scam

## Fake recruiters impersonating real employers
A July 2025 FTC alert warned that scammers pose as recruiters for major employers. They send fake interview invitations and offer letters by email or text. Real recruiters usually write from a corporate email address, not a personal Gmail or Yahoo account. Scammers often ask for your driver's license, Social Security number or bank details before any real interview, sometimes calling it "employment paperwork". Real employers interview you before asking for direct-deposit information.
Source: https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2025/07/job-scammers-are-looking-hire-you

## How fake check scams work
In a fake check scam, someone sends you a check, often for more than you are owed. They ask you to deposit it and send part of the money back or on to someone else. The reason might be job supplies, prize "taxes" or an "overpayment". Banks must make deposited funds available quickly. But the FTC warns that seeing the money in your account does not mean the check is good. A fake check can take weeks to bounce, and you must repay the bank.
Source: https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-spot-avoid-and-report-fake-check-scams

## Fake check variants aimed at job seekers
The FTC lists common fake check stories. In mystery shopping scams, you deposit a check and wire money to "retailers". In personal assistant scams, you buy gift cards and send the PINs to your "employer". In car wrap advertising scams, you pay a supposed decal installer. Other versions involve prize fees and marketplace overpayments. Never use money from a deposited check to buy gift cards or crypto, or to send a wire. If you already sent money, contact the payment service right away and report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
Source: https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-spot-avoid-and-report-fake-check-scams
